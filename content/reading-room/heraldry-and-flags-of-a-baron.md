# The arms and flags of a baron

*A baron’s arms are his own. They are granted or matriculated by the Lord Lyon and written into a public register that has been the rule of the matter since 1672. A barony adds certain marks to them, and may open the way to certain flags. This paper sets out what those are, from the Lyon Court’s guidance, the old writers on heraldry and patents the Court has issued to barons. It is as plain about what a barony does not bring with it.*

## The short answer

- **Arms belong to one person,** not to a family and not to a barony. A baron needs his own grant or matriculation from the Lord Lyon.
- **A barony shows in the arms.** For barons who had their grants before 28 November 2004 it shows by a red cap, the *chapeau*, and often a robe draped behind the shield. For those who have acquired a barony since, it shows by a helm of a baron’s degree.
- **Supporters are not automatic.** By old usage they go to the representatives of the baronial houses that sat in Parliament before 1587, and to chiefs.
- **The banner is the arms as a flag,** and belongs to whoever owns the arms. The Lyon Court’s guidance gives a baron’s as 36 inches square.
- **The standard, guidon, pennon and pinsel are each granted or assigned by the Lord Lyon.** The standard goes “only to those who have a ‘following’”, and a baron’s is three and a half metres long.
- **The Saltire is every Scot’s flag. The Lion Rampant is the Sovereign’s,** and a baron has no right to fly it.
- **The Lyon Court’s guidance survives in two versions.** They agree on sizes and differ on who may have a guidon or a pennon. Ask the Lyon Office before a flag is made.

## Arms belong to a person

“There is no such thing in Scotland as a ‘family coat of arms’”, the Lyon Court tells enquirers. A personal coat of arms “can only be borne by one person at a time”.[^1] A baron is in the same position as anyone else. His barony adds marks to his arms, as shown below, but it does not supply the arms. He must have his own.

The law is old. An Act of 1592 gave the Lyon King of Arms and his heralds power “To visite the haill armes of noblemen baronis and gentlemen borne and vsit within this realme”, and to enter them in their books.[^2] The Act of 1672 set up the register the Lyon Court still keeps, the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in Scotland, and declared that it “shall be respected as the true and unrepeallable rule of all Armes and Bearings in Scotland”. Barons are named in both Acts, and the Act of 1672 gave them a fee of their own: “20 merks by every prelate and nobleman, and 10 merks by every knight and baron”.[^3]

So a baron who wants arms petitions the Lord Lyon as anyone must. In the Court’s words, “Any person who wishes to use Arms must petition for a Grant of Arms”, or, for those who can trace their descent from an ancestor of the same name who had a grant of arms in Scotland, for a matriculation. The Court says the process “generally takes between 30 - 36 months”.[^4]

## The parts of a baron’s arms

1. **Motto**, on a scroll.
2. **Crest**, here a sheaf of holly.
3. **Helmet**. The matriculation calls it “an Helmet befitting his degree”.
4. **Mantling**, the cloth that flows from the helmet.
5. **Chapeau**, the red cap turned up with ermine: “a Chapeau Gules furred Ermine (for his Feudal Barony of Drum)”.
6. **Mantle**, the baron’s robe draped behind the shield, “fastened on the right shoulder by five spherical buttons Or”.
7. **Shield**, which is the arms proper.
8. **Supporters**, here “two savages wreathed about the head and loins with holly”.
9. **Compartment**, the ground the supporters stand on.[^5]

Any armiger may have the shield, helmet, mantling, crest and motto. The chapeau, the mantle and, for some, the supporters are what mark a baron.

### The chapeau and the mantle

The chapeau is a cap of dignity. The Lyon Court upheld a baron’s claim to it in the case of Gordon of Hallhead, Baron of Esslemont: on 4 September 1934 the Lord Lyon, in the words of Sir Thomas Innes of Learney, “found the claim established, and awarded the chapeau”.[^6] The mantle is the baron’s robe, shown in the painting as it was once worn. Both are treated at length in [Robes, chapeau and insignia of the baronage](/reading-room/robes-and-insignia/).

Both belong to grants made on petitions lodged before the appointed day, 28 November 2004. The Lord Lyon’s guidance of 5 January 2010 says that barons “who were granted a chapeau Gules or, it may be, a mantle before the appointed day may, of course, continue to display these additaments”, and proposes that heirs who succeed to such baronies “should continue to enjoy a red chapeau as an appropriate additament, and also a mantle if that has been granted before”. Those who acquire a barony after 28 November 2004 “will be granted a helm appropriate to their degree, that is, to the dignity of baron”.[^7]

### The helm

The old writers gave a Scottish baron no helmet of his own. Sir George Mackenzie in 1680 set out the English rule and the French, and the helm he gives “To a Barron”, carried “half side-wise, half in Front”, is the French one. Alexander Nisbet gave the barred helmet in profile to the peers: “by our practice, a knight has a fore-standing helmet open, and our dignified nobility a side-standing helmet with bars”. Neither says what a Scottish baron should have.[^8]

The Lyon Court’s patents use a formula instead. The Drum matriculation of 1992 has “an Helmet befitting his degree”, set on the chapeau. The Hartsyde document described below, for a barony acquired in 2010, has “an Helm befitting the holder of the dignity of baron”, with no chapeau or mantle. That is the guidance of 2010 at work.

### Supporters

Supporters are the figures that stand on either side of the shield. Mackenzie claimed them for the old barons in 1680: “I crave liberty to assert, that all our Chiefs of Families, and old Barons in Scotland, may use Supporters”. His reason was that barons “were Members of Parliament with us, as such, and never lost that Priviledge, though for their convenience, they were allowed to be represented by two of their number”. Among his examples is “Irwin of Drum, two Savages, wreathed about head and loyns with Hollin, and bearing Battons in their hands”: the supporters in the Drum matriculation of 1992, shown above.[^9]

The Lyon Court came to apply that as a rule with a date. In a note of 27 June 1821 the Lyon-Depute, George Tait, listed among those entitled the “Lawful heirs-male of the bodies of the smaller Barons, who had the full right of free barony (not mere freeholders) prior to 1587”, and added a warning: “the want of usage is a strong presumption against the right.”[^10] In 1838 Sir Thomas Burnet of Leys received supporters as “the male representative of one of the minor Barons of Scotland prior to the year 1587”.[^11]

So supporters go with an old baronial house, not with the holding of a barony as such. The guidance of 2010 keeps the rule: “Armigers formerly entitled to a chapeau Azure, or to supporters as representatives of baronial houses who had a seat in Parliament until 1587, continue to be so entitled.”[^7]

## The five flags

“The other principal sign of honour”, Nisbet wrote in 1722, “is the ensign; under which general name, are comprehended standards, banners, pennons, gideons, and gonfanouns.”[^12] The Lyon Court’s guidance describes five: the banner, the standard, the guidon, the pennon and the pinsel.

Its guidance on them survives in two versions. One is a printed leaflet, “Information Leaflet No. 3 … Scottish Heraldic Flags”, in feet and inches. The other is a page of the Court’s former website, “Further Guidance on Flags”, in metric measures. Neither carries a date, but the leaflet is the older: it names the Secretary of State for Scotland where the web page names the First Minister.[^13] They agree on sizes and differ on who may have two of the flags, as shown below. Both call barons “Feudal Barons”. The Court’s present website has no page on flags.

### The banner

The banner is the arms themselves made into a flag. The Lyon Court calls it “the personal flag of the owner of a coat of arms”, showing the arms “granted by the Lord Lyon or inherited in right of an ancestor”. It is “often wrongly called a ‘Standard’”. The guidance sets it apart from the other four, which it lists as “Special Heraldic Flags”, each granted, assigned or allotted by the Lord Lyon.[^14]

The rule for drawing it is strict. “The coat of arms fills the banner right to its edges, as though it were a rectangular shield. It is quite wrong to use a banner of a plain colour with the owner’s arms on a shield in the middle.” Helmet, crest, motto and supporters are left off.[^14]

The banner stands for its owner. “No one else may use it. Flown over the house it denotes that the armiger is there”. For a house flag the proportions are five to four.[^14]

For personal use the size goes by rank. The leaflet gives “Barons and Feudal Barons : 36 Inches square”. The web page has “Baronets and Feudal Barons : 0.90 square metres”, which a later copy corrects to “0.90 metres square”. A banner made to be carried in procession is taller: “Feudal Barons : 90 centimetres wide x 115 centimetres high”, or 36 inches by 45.[^15]

### The standard

The standard is the long tapering flag, and it is not for every armiger. It is “granted by the Lord Lyon only to those who have a ‘following’, such as Clan Chiefs, because it is a ‘Headquarters’ flag. It is used to mark the assembly point or Headquarters of the Clan or following, and does not necessarily denote the presence of the Standard’s owner as the personal banner does.”[^16]

The guidance describes its parts. Next to the pole, in the hoist, old standards showed the Saltire and newer ones often show the owner’s arms. The rest is divided lengthways into tracts of the owner’s livery colours, on which are set the crest and badges, “separated by transverse bands bearing the owner’s motto or slogan”. And there is a mark of rank in the shape of the end: “Those of peers and barons have the ends split into two and rounded.”[^16]

The Association’s Armorial has an example. The Letters Patent of Charles Russell Clayton Ross, Baron of Biggar, granted on a petition of 5 November 2004 and dated 23 October 2007, blazon his badge and crest “upon a Standard three and a half metres in length of four tracts Argent and Gules, split at the end”, with the Saltire in the hoist and the motto “in letters Argent upon two transverse bands Azure”. The patent limits the standard, with the chapeau, mantle and badge, to the petitioner and his successors in the arms “who are in the right of the barony of Biggar”.[^17]

On length the two versions agree. The leaflet gives “Knights and Barons : 4 yards”, which is 3.66 metres, and the web page gives “Knights and Feudal Barons : 3½ metres”: its metric table is the leaflet’s table of yards rounded to the half metre. The Biggar standard is exactly that length. A later copy of the web page adds a caution. It reads “Knights and, where a Standard has been granted, Feudal Barons”.[^18]

### The guidon

The guidon is a shorter flag of the same shape, 2.40 metres or 8 feet long, ending in “a round, unsplit end”. It carries the crest or badge in the hoist and the motto in the fly. The two versions disagree on who may have one. The metric page assigns it “to those individuals who qualify for a grant of supporters to their Arms and to other individuals who have a following”. The leaflet assigns it “to Lairds who have a following, as for Standards, but are of non-baronial tenure”.[^19]

### The pennon

The pennon is half the length of the guidon, 120 centimetres or 4 feet. The metric page says it “is assigned by the Lord Lyon to any armiger who wishes to apply for it”; the leaflet says it is assigned “in very rare cases and circumstances nowadays”.[^20]

The Armorial has one of these too. The Lyon Court document shown on the Roll for the Baron of Hartsyde, in the name of Lord Lyon Sellar, adds to his arms a “Pennon 120 centimetres long”, with “the Arms of the Petitioner in the hoist” and his motto.[^21]

### The pinsel

The pinsel is a small triangular flag, 60 centimetres high and 135 long, and it is a chief’s. It is “the flag denoting a person to whom a Clan Chief has delegated authority for a particular occasion”. It bears the chief’s crest within a strap and buckle and a circlet with his title, and “On top of this circlet is set the owner’s coronet of rank or his baronial cap.” It is not a flag for barons generally: “This flag is allotted only to Chiefs or very special Chieftain-Barons for practical use, and only upon the specific authority of the Lord Lyon King of Arms.”[^22]

On sizes the two agree, the metric figures being the old ones rounded. On who may have a guidon or a pennon they do not, and the Lyon Office is the only authority on which rule now applies. A baron who wants a flag should put the question to the Lyon Clerk first.

## The Saltire and the Lion Rampant

A baron has no special right to either national flag, and needs none for the first. The Saltire is “the correct flag for all Scots or Scottish corporate bodies to fly”, and the leaflet adds that it “may be freely flown by any Scot”.[^23]

The Lion Rampant is another matter. In the Lyon Court’s words, “This is NOT a national flag and its use by citizens and corporate bodies is entirely wrong.” It “is the Scottish Royal banner”, kept for the Sovereign and a few great officers who represent the Sovereign.[^24] J. H. Stevenson called its common use, in 1914, “by far the most outrageous infringement of the law in Scotland”.[^25]

## Getting arms and flags today

The Lyon Court still paints these flags. Its note of fees for April 2026 lists “Additional painting fees” for a “Standard or Guidon” from £295, a “Pinsel” at £380, a “Pennon or Pennoncel” at £273 and a “Banner” from £147, with a “Badge” at £189.[^26] In the Biggar patent the standard is both blazoned in the text and painted in the margin.

A baron who wants a flag has three steps to take. He should hold arms of his own. He should ask the Lyon Office which flags he may have and in what size. And he should have the flag made to the blazon in his patent, with the arms filling a banner to its edges.

## Five things often said

**“A barony comes with a coat of arms.”** It does not. Arms are one person’s, by grant or matriculation, and each baron needs his own.[^1]

**“Every baron may have supporters.”** No. The old rule gives them to the representatives of the baronial houses that sat in Parliament before 1587, and Tait warned in 1821 that “the want of usage is a strong presumption against the right.”[^10]

**“A baron has a right to a standard.”** A standard is “granted by the Lord Lyon only to those who have a ‘following’”. Barons have been granted them, as at Biggar, and the guidance gives a baron’s length, but in its later wording only “where a Standard has been granted”.[^16]

**“A shield on a coloured flag is a banner.”** The Lyon Court calls that “quite wrong”. The arms must fill the flag.[^14]

**“Any Scot may fly the Lion Rampant.”** No. The Saltire is the flag for all Scots. The Lion Rampant is the Sovereign’s.[^24]
