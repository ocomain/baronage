# Lairds, lords and barons: what the words meant

*“Laird” is the Scottish word most often misunderstood — taken for a rank, sold with souvenir plots, confused with “lord”. The records say something simpler: laird and lord are one word, laird was never a rank, and what turned a laird into a baron was a Crown charter, not a name.*

## One word, two spellings

“Laird” is not a separate rank from “lord”; it is the same word. Old English *hlāford* — literally the “loaf-ward”, the keeper of the bread, the head of a household — became *laverd* in early Scots and English alike. In the south it contracted to *lord*; in Scotland it also contracted to *lard*, then *laird*. The Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue records *lard* from 1379 (“Schir William the Lyndesay lard of the Byres”) and gives its first sense as “one who has dominion over others; a prince, chief or lord” — it was even used of Christ (“the yhere of oure lard”, 1389).[^1] Sir George Mackenzie, Lord Advocate under Charles II, put it bluntly in 1680: “a Laird, in effect, is but the corrupt Word of a Lord”.[^2]

## The same thing happened in England

In medieval England every master of a household or estate was somebody’s “lord”: the lord of the manor, the landlord, the “lord” of a monastery’s tenants. Only later did “Lord” harden into a rank reserved for the peerage. The dictionaries date the sense “an owner of land, houses, etc.” (the sense in *landlord*) to about 1300, and the use of “Lord” as a formal address for peers below ducal rank to the 1540s.[^3] England kept one spelling and split the meaning. Scotland kept the meaning and split the spelling — though not cleanly into rank on one side and land on the other. “Laird” stayed with the estate and its owner, baron or not. “Lord” went two ways at once. From the mid-fifteenth century it was the style of the Lords of Parliament, the personal peerage. But it was also, and had been for longer, the style of the territorial lordship: the free lordship erected by Crown charter, *dominus de X*, “Lord of X / Lord X”, a lord in the baronage of Scotland ranking above a baron, but non-peerage. Many of those lordships were afterwards raised into Lords of Parliament. Others never were — most obviously where the holder already sat in Parliament under a higher dignity and had no need of the further honour — and they continued as lordships of the nobility in their own right. A “Lord of X / Lord X” in a Scottish charter is therefore not necessarily a peer, and in Scotland “lord” for titled nobility never became, as it did in England, a word the peerage owned.

## Who was a laird?

A laird was a landholder known by the name of his land. The Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue defines the Scots sense as “a landowner who was a tenant-in-chief of the sovereign, a baron or a freeholder; after the 14th c., chiefly or only one of the smaller barons or landowners”, and adds that the word was “from an early date applied only to the ‘smaller barons’ or smaller landowners generally, as opposed to the greater or titled barons or ‘lords’”.[^1] The records show the distinction in use. In 1467 Parliament listed “for the barons … the lord Lindissay, the lord Lyle, the lard of Caldorwod” — two peers and a laird, all sitting as barons. In 1625 the Privy Council named “the lairds of Keir, Tracquair [etc.], for the small barronis”.[^1] Mackenzie again: “such as did hold their Lands of the Prince, were called Lairds, but such as held their Lands of a Subject, though they were large, and their Superior very noble, were only called Good-men”.[^2]

So in Scots usage “laird” was a catch-all for the landed man below the peerage. A baron was a laird; so was a freeholder who held of the Crown but had no barony; and by the eighteenth century, as the Scottish National Dictionary notes, so was the owner “of an estate of land of any size from the largest to a single farm”, the equivalent of the English squire — “every one who possessed a piece of land, however small, rent free, was called a laird” (1807).[^4] Lord Kames observed in 1747 that “a Scotch Laird has come to be in some Measure a Term of Reproach, like a French Marquis, or a German Baron” — the word had spread far beyond the baronage.[^4]

## What made a laird a baron

Not the word, but the tenure. A baron was a laird whose lands the Crown had erected *in liberam baroniam*, with a baron court and jurisdiction; Mackenzie: “such Feus as had a Jurisdiction annext to them, a Barony, as we call it, do ennoble; for Baronies are establisht only by the Prince’s Erection or Confirmation”.[^2] The records kept the two apart even when speech did not: the Act of 1428 excuses “the small barons and freeholders” from Parliament;[^5] the Act of 1587 gives the vote at shire elections to freeholders of forty shillings land held of the king;[^6] a Banffshire sheriff in 1713 ordered production of charters “that it may be known who are barons and who have power to vote”.[^7] As Lord Lyon Innes of Learney wrote, “Freeholders were not Barons and everybody recognised that. Heraldically Freeholders are not allowed the insignia of Barons.”[^7] (See [The Lord Lyon’s Case for the Baronage](/reading-room/innes-of-learney-1945/), section 3.)

The Latin of the sheriff-court rolls is the clearest guide. Answering the head court, a Crown vassal was entered “*Intrat A, Comes de B*” (Earl of B), “*Intrat A, Dominus de B*” (Lord of B) or “*Intrat A, Baro de B*” (Baron of B), never “A, who holds the lands of B in free barony”.[^7] William Borthwick set out the same rule for Parliament in 1775: “if he held his lands in libero comitatu, he was marked down Comes; if he held his lands in libero dominio, he was marked Dominus; if he held them only in libera baronia, he was marked Baro.”[^8] Comes, Dominus, Baro, laird: the first three were ranks of holding, the fourth was what the neighbours called all of them.

Arms mattered apart from the barony, too. A laird who matriculated a coat of arms with the Lord Lyon, without ever holding his lands in free barony, was armigerous, and so counted among the untitled nobility of Scotland — the equivalent, in England, of the gentry: armigerous, but bearing no title.

## In the Crown’s own words

Some entries in the Register of the Great Seal simply name a man as baron of his lands.[^9] Others say what the erection gave.[^10]

On 20 June 1589 James VI made the abbey lands of Culross a barony for James Colville of Easter Wemyss, with “titulum, honorem et statum liberi baronis ratione dictarum terrarum”: the title, honour and status of a free baron, by reason of the lands. Colville was to bear a free baron’s badge and arms and be called baron of Culross.[^11]

He was not then a peer. *The Scots Peerage* dates his lordship of Parliament to 1604.[^12]

Spynie shows the same words read two ways. Alexander Lindsay’s charter of 6 May 1590 granted him “Titulum, Honorem, Ordinem, et Statum liberi Baronis”, the holders to be called Barons of Spynie for ever.[^13]

In the peerage claim of 1785, notes of Lord Mansfield’s speech record that the claimant “now admitted that this charter has nothing to do with Peerage”. The words of a lordship of Parliament appear in the Act of ratification of 1592 and the charter of 1593.[^14]

John Riddell disagreed with Mansfield in 1842. In his reading, “title, honour, and rank of a free Baron” then described a peer. He cited Sir James Balfour’s record that Lindsay was made a Lord of Parliament on 4 November 1590.[^15]

On either reading, the words were the Crown’s own.

## “Of that Ilk”: the territorial designation

The sheriff-court roll entered a Crown vassal as *Baro de B*; the neighbours called him the laird of B; he signed himself “A of B”, or, where surname and estate coincided, “A of that Ilk”. That signature is the territorial designation: a place-name adjoined to the surname, recognised by the Lord Lyon as part of the surname and entered in the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in Scotland. It designates a person; it is neither a title of dignity nor a barony. Lord Lyon Sellar’s guidance of 5 January 2010 gives the modern test, the “classic case” being “ownership of an ‘estate’, or farm or, at the very least, a house with policies extending to five acres or thereby, outwith a burgh”.[^16]

The custom is older than the test. The Lyon King of Arms Act 1672 “declares that it is only allowed for noblemen ([peers](/scottish-baronies-explained/#term-peerage), ‘the Nobility’ in its narrow English sense) and bishops to subscribe by their titles, and that all others shall subscribe their christened names … with their surnames, and may, if they please, adject the designations of their lands prefixing the word ‘of’ to the said designations”. The words were repealed in 1995; the thing they describe survives.[^17] Sir Crispin Agnew of Lochnaw wrote that “once a territorial designation has been consistently used over a few generations (and probably recorded in the Lyon Court books), then it becomes separable from the land and continues as that family’s proper surname”. After roughly three generations the “of” has become the name.[^18]

What it is not is plain. A souvenir plot confers nothing: the Court of the Lord Lyon calls such plots “insufficient to bring anyone within the jurisdiction of the Lord Lyon King of Arms”,[^19] and they cannot be entered in the Land Register.[^20] Nor is the designation a title. HM Passport Office records that “The Lord Lyon King of Arms has confirmed that for Scottish Lairds it is not necessary for the words ‘Laird of’ to appear on any part of the passport”; the Passport Office’s form for the surname field is “(surname) of (territorial designation)”.[^21]

Designation and barony can coexist, but are decided separately. In *Kerr of Ardgowan v Lord Lyon* 2009 SLT 759 the petitioner was recognised as Baron of Ardgowan, the designation being a separate question; the Extra Division held that the Lord Lyon “does indeed enjoy a wide discretion in deciding whether or not to accept a change of name for entry in the Public Register in question”.[^22] A baron who owns the historic lands of his barony may be “Surname of X, Baron of X”: one is his name, the other his dignity. Three things, not one: laird is what the neighbours called a landholder; the designation is the Lyon Court’s formal recognition of that “of”; the barony is a Crown erection. See the FAQ: [What is a territorial designation, and is it the same as a barony?](/scottish-baronies-explained/#what-is-a-territorial-designation-td-and-is-it-the-same-as-a-barony).

## Laird today

Laird remains a description, not a title. HM Passport Office records that “The Lord Lyon King of Arms has confirmed that for Scottish Lairds it is not necessary for the words ‘Laird of’ to appear on any part of the passport”,[^21] and the Court of the Lord Lyon said so in terms on its former website: laird “is description rather than a title” and “is not appropriate for the owner of a normal residential property, far less the owner of a small souvenir plot of land” (see [Scottish Baronies, Explained](/scottish-baronies-explained/)).[^23] A souvenir plot of a few square feet does not make its buyer a laird in any sense the Lyon Court recognises: the Court’s current guidance on petitioning for arms states that such plots are “insufficient to bring anyone within the jurisdiction of the Lord Lyon King of Arms”,[^19] and they cannot be registered in the Land Register at all.[^20] A barony is different in kind — a dignity preserved by statute[^24] — which is why the passport guidance provides for “BARON OF (territorial designation)”, where the Passport Office’s “territorial designation” means the name of the barony, but not for “Laird of”.[^21]
