# Lairds, lords and barons: what the words meant

*“Laird” is the Scottish word most often misunderstood — taken for a rank, sold with souvenir plots, confused with “lord”. The records say something simpler: laird and lord are one word, laird was never a rank, and what turned a laird into a baron was a Crown charter, not a name.*

## One word, two spellings

“Laird” is not a separate rank from “lord”; it is the same word. Old English *hlāford* — literally the “loaf-ward”, the keeper of the bread, the head of a household — became *laverd* in early Scots and English alike. In the south it contracted to *lord*; in Scotland it also contracted to *lard*, then *laird*. The Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue records *lard* from 1379 (“Schir William the Lyndesay lard of the Byres”) and gives its first sense as “one who has dominion over others; a prince, chief or lord” — it was even used of Christ (“the yhere of oure lard”, 1389).[^1] Sir George Mackenzie, Lord Advocate under Charles II, put it bluntly in 1680: “a Laird, in effect, is but the corrupt Word of a Lord”.[^2]

## The same thing happened in England

In medieval England every master of a household or estate was somebody’s “lord”: the lord of the manor, the landlord, the “lord” of a monastery’s tenants. Only later did “Lord” harden into a rank reserved for the peerage. The dictionaries date the sense “an owner of land, houses, etc.” (the sense in *landlord*) to about 1300, and the use of “Lord” as a formal address for peers below ducal rank to the 1540s.[^3] England kept one spelling and split the meaning; Scotland kept the meaning and split the spelling, so that “lord” drifted upwards to the Lords of Parliament while “laird” stayed with the estate.

## Who was a laird?

A laird was a landholder known by the name of his land. The Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue defines the Scots sense as “a landowner who was a tenant-in-chief of the sovereign, a baron or a freeholder; after the 14th c., chiefly or only one of the smaller barons or landowners”, and adds that the word was “from an early date applied only to the ‘smaller barons’ or smaller landowners generally, as opposed to the greater or titled barons or ‘lords’”.[^1] The records show the distinction in use. In 1467 Parliament listed “for the barons … the lord Lindissay, the lord Lyle, the lard of Caldorwod” — two peers and a laird, all sitting as barons. In 1625 the Privy Council named “the lairds of Keir, Tracquair [etc.], for the small barronis”.[^1] Mackenzie again: “such as did hold their Lands of the Prince, were called Lairds, but such as held their Lands of a Subject, though they were large, and their Superior very noble, were only called Good-men”.[^2]

So in Scots usage “laird” was a catch-all for the landed man below the peerage. A baron was a laird; so was a freeholder who held of the Crown but had no barony; and by the eighteenth century, as the Scottish National Dictionary notes, so was the owner “of an estate of land of any size from the largest to a single farm”, the equivalent of the English squire — “every one who possessed a piece of land, however small, rent free, was called a laird” (1807).[^4] Lord Kames observed in 1747 that “a Scotch Laird has come to be in some Measure a Term of Reproach, like a French Marquis, or a German Baron” — the word had spread far beyond the baronage.[^4]

## What made a laird a baron

Not the word, but the tenure. A baron was a laird whose lands the Crown had erected *in liberam baroniam*, with a baron court and jurisdiction; Mackenzie: “such Feus as had a Jurisdiction annext to them, a Barony, as we call it, do ennoble; for Baronies are establisht only by the Prince’s Erection or Confirmation”.[^2] The records kept the two apart even when speech did not: the Act of 1428 excuses “the small barons and freeholders” from Parliament;[^5] the Act of 1587 gives the vote at shire elections to freeholders of forty shillings land held of the king;[^6] a Banffshire sheriff in 1713 ordered production of charters “that it may be known who are barons and who have power to vote”.[^7] As Lord Lyon Innes of Learney wrote, “Freeholders were not Barons and everybody recognised that. Heraldically Freeholders are not allowed the insignia of Barons.”[^7] (See [The Lord Lyon’s Case for the Baronage](/reading-room/innes-of-learney-1945/), section 3.)

The Latin of the sheriff-court rolls is the clearest guide. Answering the head court, a Crown vassal was entered “*Intrat A, Comes de B*” (Earl of B), “*Intrat A, Dominus de B*” (Lord of B) or “*Intrat A, Baro de B*” (Baron of B), never “A, who holds the lands of B in free barony”.[^7] William Borthwick set out the same rule in 1775: “if he held his lands in libero comitatu, he was marked Comes; if he held his lands in libero dominio, he was marked Dominus; if he held them only in libera baronia, he was marked Baro.”[^8] Comes, Dominus, Baro, laird: the first three were ranks of holding, the fourth was what the neighbours called all of them.

## Laird today

Laird remains a description, not a title. HM Passport Office records that “The Lord Lyon King of Arms has confirmed that for Scottish Lairds it is not necessary for the words ‘Laird of’ to appear on any part of the passport”,[^9] and the Court of the Lord Lyon said so in terms on its former website: laird “is description rather than a title” and “is not appropriate for the owner of a normal residential property, far less the owner of a small souvenir plot of land” (see [Scottish Baronies, Explained](/scottish-baronies-explained/)).[^10] A souvenir plot of a few square feet does not make its buyer a laird in any sense the Lyon Court recognises: the Court’s current guidance on petitioning for arms states that such plots are “insufficient to bring anyone within the jurisdiction of the Lord Lyon King of Arms”,[^11] and they cannot be registered in the Land Register at all.[^12] A barony is different in kind — a dignity preserved by statute[^13] — which is why the passport guidance provides for “BARON OF (territorial designation)” but not for “Laird of”.[^9]
