# Barons in the Gazette

*Between 1951 and 2005 the Court of the Lord Lyon published thirty-six notices in The Edinburgh Gazette, each a list of names it had officially recognised. Most of those named were not barons. A dagger against a name marked a Minor Baron. Not one notice uses the word “feudal”.*

## The Gazette notices

The Edinburgh Gazette is Scotland’s edition of The Gazette, the United Kingdom’s official public record.[^1] From 1951 to 2005 the Court of the Lord Lyon published its recognitions there, in thirty-six notices.[^2] Each named the people “Officially Recognised by the Lord Lyon King of Arms in matriculating for them Arms ‘of that description’”: recognised, that is, in the name printed beside their entry.[^3]

The first notice, of 16 January 1951, continued an unpublished roll “for the years 1866-1948 compiled for the Lord Chamberlain’s Department in 1948”.[^4] The notices kept no fixed schedule; once almost six years passed without one.[^5] The Lyon Clerk signed each.[^6] The last appeared on 19 August 2005, and no explanation of why the series stopped has been found.[^7]

These were official acts, and the Gazette treats them as such. Its own record files the notices under “State”, as “Change of Name and/or Arms”.[^8] On the printed page they are headed “Court of the Lord Lyon” from 1968.[^9]

## A designation is not a barony

A territorial designation is the “of X” added to a surname, taken from land its owner holds. It does not make anyone a baron. Most of the people named in the Gazette notices were not barons: in the notice of 15 March 1968 only four of the sixteen entries carry the baron’s mark.[^10] The right goes back to the Lyon King of Arms Act 1672, which let anyone who was not a nobleman or bishop “adject the designations of their lands prefixing the word ‘of’”.[^11] How that practice developed is set out more fully in [Lairds, lords and barons](/reading-room/lairds-lords-and-barons/). The Gazette notices record the next step: the Lord Lyon’s official recognition of the name with its designation, once arms have been matriculated in it.[^3]

From 24 January 1967 the notices carried a new note: “† indicates that the Arms have been matriculated with chapeau in respect of being a Minor Baron.”[^12] No entry in that issue actually carried the mark yet. The first to do so was the notice of 15 March 1968, where four names were daggered: Forbes of Brux, Gordon of Abergeldie, Innes of Edingight and Vans of Barnbarroch.[^10]

The chapeau itself, the baron’s cap of maintenance, had been settled as the baronial mark by the Lord Lyon’s decision of 4 September 1934, in the petition of Gordon of Hallhead, Baron of Esslemont, told in full in [The Esslemont petition of 1934](/reading-room/esslemont-petition-1934/).[^13] “Minor” in Minor Baron does not mean small. It means the barons below the peerage, the barones minores. In 1943 the Lyon Court declared that the minor barons “are, and have been both in this nobiliary Court and in the Court of Session recognised as a ‘titled nobility’”. That was not a new finding but an old position made plain, as [Untitled or titled nobility?](/reading-room/untitled-in-1930-titled-in-1943/) sets out.[^14] The mark itself did not stay fixed. It was a dagger in 1968, a different, curled glyph in 1984, and a slashed “ø” in several notices between 1986 and 2000, among them the one of 17 May 1988.[^15] Through all of that the note explaining it barely changed.[^15]

## Three ways a baron appears

| Pattern | Example | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Designation the same as the barony: the name and the mark alone | ø*Irvine of Drum | 26 April 1994 |
| Designation different from the barony, both printed | †Teall of Teallach Baron of Huntly | 4 April 1997 |
| No designation, the barony printed on its own | øHolman, Baron of Rickarton | 17 May 1988 |

Most entries take the first form. From 1988 it is shorthand: when a baron’s designation was already his barony’s name, the Gazette printed the name once with the mark, as with “†Sturzeneggar of Arran” in 2002.[^16] In full that would read “Sturzeneggar of Arran, Baron of Arran”. Irvine of Drum shows it. His matriculation of 25 November 1992 names him “Baron of Drum in the County of Aberdeen”. The Gazette of 26 April 1994 prints only “ø*Irvine of Drum”, with the same date and register page.[^17] The mark saved printing the word twice.

Before 1988 the same form tells less. The Gazette then printed the name and the mark alone, whatever the barony was called. “†Forbes of Brux” in 1968 was Baron of Brux. But “Humphrey of Dinnet”, marked a minor baron in the notice of 17 January 1984, was Baron of Ochtercoull, and “*†Cumyn of Rattray” in 1977 was Baron of Broadland.[^18] In those notices the mark shows that a man was a minor baron, not of which barony. The first notice found to name a barony is that of 17 May 1988.

Sometimes the two differed, and then the Gazette printed both. Dennistoun Gordon Teall’s designation was Teallach, not Huntly, so the 1997 notice reads “†Teall of Teallach Baron of Huntly”.[^19] The 2002 notice does the same: “*†McLeod of Rothie, Baron of Rothienorman”.[^20] Huntly and Arran are themselves non-peerage earldoms, a separate subject covered in [Non-peerage earldoms](/reading-room/non-peerage-earldoms/).

Some barons carried no separate territorial designation at all, and for them the Gazette printed the barony on its own next to the mark. The earliest found is “øHolman, Baron of Rickarton” in the notice of 17 May 1988.[^21] The 2002 notice gives “†Thacker, Baron of Fetternear”, on the same page as the Sturzeneggar and McLeod of Rothie entries above.[^22]

## Not every baron was gazetted

The Gazette is a partial record. A name appears only where arms were matriculated in it, and only while the series ran. Some barons were gazetted and others were not. That was so in earlier times too. The first notice continued a roll for 1866 to 1948 compiled for the Lord Chamberlain’s Department, and no Gazette notice of that roll has been found. Barons recognised in those years, such as Gordon of Hallhead, Baron of Esslemont, in 1934 and Wauchope of Niddrie in 1945, appear in no notice.[^23] Nothing was gazetted after 2005, so barons recognised in Letters Patent of 2007, 2010 or 2018 appear in no notice.[^24]

So a Gazette entry is good evidence, but its absence proves nothing. The [Roll of Scottish Barons](https://roll.baronage.com) accepts other Lyon Court and government records as well.

## Not once “feudal”

The word “feudal” does not appear in any of the thirty-six notices. Every notice page, from 1951 to 2005, was searched for it. It was not found once, not even in the note that explains the Minor Baron’s mark.[^25]

The Lyon Court’s own word, in the Gazette, was always “Minor Baron”. When the series began in January 1951, Sir Thomas Innes of Learney had been Lord Lyon for some years. The notices are signed by his Lyon Clerk, not by him.[^4][^26] About five years earlier, in 1945, Innes of Learney had given his own paper to the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland, and titled it “The Robes of the Feudal Baronage of Scotland”.[^27] The Gazette never used his word. Why “feudal” is the wrong word for a barony today is set out in [Scottish Baronies, Explained](/scottish-baronies-explained/#is-scottish-feudal-barony-or-feudal-baron-or-feudal-title-the-correct-term-today).
