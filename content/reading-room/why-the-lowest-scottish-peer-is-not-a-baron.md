# Why is the lowest Scottish peer a Lord of Parliament, not a baron?

*In England a baron is the lowest rank of the peerage. The Peerage of Scotland has no such rank: its lowest is the Lord of Parliament, and “baron” stayed with the baronage. This paper shows how the line was drawn, from the Great Seal, and why it matters to the standing of a Scottish baron.*

## The short answer

- **The Peerage of Scotland has no baron.** Its ranks are duke, marquis, earl, viscount and Lord of Parliament. A woman holding the lowest rank sat in the Lords as “Lady Saltoun of Abernethy”, not “Baroness”.
- **The baronage came first.** “Baron” was the name of the whole order. Sir Alexander Forbes held a free barony by 1429 and became a lord of parliament only in 1444 or 1445.
- **The honour was added.** A Great Seal charter of 1583 makes Andrew Keith lord and baron of Dingwall, “who should have the honour of a lord of Parliament”.
- **Sometimes the honour was in the land.** At Torphichen the title of Lord of Parliament was annexed to part of a barony granted to “heirs and assignees”: “a territorial Peerage”, the peerage lawyer Riddell called it. Its holder was Baron of Torphichen by the barony and Lord Torphichen by the honour, and a notice of 1862 used both.
- **So a Scottish baron is no lesser copy of an English peer.** In England the word names a rank of the peerage. In Scotland it names a dignity of its own, which the Act of 2000 keeps by name.

## “Baron” meant the whole order

Sir George Mackenzie, Lord Advocate, wrote in 1680: “Under the Word Baron all our Nobility are comprehended”.[^1] A Lord Lyon said the same two centuries later: “The Earls belonged to the order of Barons.”[^2] Parliament counted the barons in the nobility: “every noble man, such as an earl, lord, knight and baron” in 1540, “a part of the nobility” in 1567, and “the nobility, earls, lords and barons” in 1592.[^3] Tenure gave the name. William Borthwick wrote in 1775 that a man holding in free earldom was marked earl, in free lordship lord, and in free barony baron.[^4]

## An honour added to the lordship

The Act of 1428 excused the small barons from Parliament and kept the great men to it, the “lords of parliament” among them, summoned by the King’s “special precept”.[^5] The first named creations follow in the 1440s. Forbes is the clear case: a free barony in 1429, the honour of Parliament in 1444 or 1445.[^6]

The Register of the Great Seal shows that lordship and the honour of Parliament were separate things. A charter could give the one without the other.

> “in free lordship and barony … the said Andrew to be called lord and baron of Dingwall, who should have the honour of a lord of Parliament”
>
> Charter of 18 March 1583, in modern English[^7]

- **1583, Dingwall.** A lord and baron by charter, with the honour named as something further.
- **1587, Paisley.** A free temporal lordship and barony, with “the title and honour of a free baron and lord of parliaments”.[^8]
- **1591, Balquhidder.** Sir John Murray, a knight, in free lordship. No honour of Parliament.[^9]
- **1631, Fenton.** James Maxwell of Innerwick, in free lordship and barony. No honour of Parliament.[^10]

It is often said that the only lords are peers. The Register answers it. Balquhidder and Fenton were erected into free lordships with no honour of Parliament, and their holders were lords of those lands and nothing more. Dingwall’s charter adds the honour as a further clause. Torphichen shows a third kind of lord. Before his lordship became a hereditary peerage in 1564, Sir James Sandilands was Lord of St John by his office as Preceptor of the Knights, and the Preceptors held it for their lives, by election and “investiture in the fief”. So “lord” alone did not make a peer. The peerage rank needed its fuller name, Lord of Parliament, because lords held territorially in the baronage. It was at first an honour added to such a lordship, and only later given by patent alone: in 1627 Sir Thomas Fairfax, an Englishman, was made Lord Fairfax of Cameron with no lands.[^11]

## Torphichen: the honour in the land

At Torphichen the barony and the lordship of Parliament were one estate. In January 1564 Sir James Sandilands, Preceptor of the Knights of St John in Scotland, resigned the Order’s lands into Queen Mary’s hands. She granted them back to him “and his heirs and assignees”, for 10,000 crowns of the sun, and incorporated them “into the free barony of Torphichen”. The charter names no title. It calls the erection a free barony, not a lordship, and its grantee “James, lord of St John’s”: the Preceptor’s style, for the Preceptors, Riddell notes, had held the peerage for their lives, “after their election, but more especially investiture in the fief”.[^12] In 1606 the charter was accepted “as the sole evidence creating the lordship and carrying with it the dignity of Lord Torphichen”.[^13] Riddell, writing on peerage law in 1833, read the record so: the grant “is merely that of a common barony, but with higher rights and privileges”; the heir of 1597 “assumes the dignity, on vesting himself in the fief, by retour”; and the barony “passed like a common property, under a limitation applicable to land, carrying the Peerage along with it”.[^14]

When Charles I resumed the church lands, the fourth Lord feared for the superiorities of his barony. An award of the King in 1636, with the force of an Act of Parliament, kept them “within that … portion of it in which the title and dignity of Lords of Parliament subsists, and to which the title of Lord of Parliament is annexed”.[^15] The peerage stood in the land: a lordship of Parliament annexed to part of a barony, held of the Crown by heirs and assignees. “Is not this a territorial Peerage?”, Riddell asked: a title that follows “the fate of the land, in which it subsists, and without which, like the head and members, it cannot subsist”. Sixty years later Stevenson’s legal encyclopaedia laid down that a dignity “is not now capable of transference”, and added: “The barony of Torphichen is possibly an exception.”[^16]

The holder carried two titles, as Forbes did: the barony made him Baron of Torphichen, the honour made him Lord Torphichen. Neither is a form of the other. When the tenth Lord died in March 1862, the London press used both. Its heading was “Death of Lord Torphichen”; its text recorded “James Sandilands, Baron of Torphichen, in West Lothian, in the peerage of Scotland”, who had “succeeded to the ancient barony on the death of his cousin, the ninth lord”, and whose heir was “Master of Torphichen”.[^17] The barony is named first and the peerage after it, in one sentence, for one man.

## What the Union changed

England took another road. There “baron” became the lowest rank of the peerage, and at Westminster a baron was a peer. The Peerage of Scotland closed at the Union of 1707, and no Scottish peerage has been created since. The Stair Encyclopaedia puts it in a line: the lowest rank in the peerage was “lord” in Scotland before the Union, and has been “baron” since.[^18]

That is why the King’s Scale of 1905 lists “Barons of Scotland” among the peers: it uses the British name for the lords of parliament.[^19] The holders themselves stayed Lords, and the women show it best. The Peerage Act 1963 gave a woman holding a peerage in her own right a seat in the Lords.[^20] Within months the House had “Baroness Strange of Knokin”, in an English barony, and “The Lady Kinloss”, in a Scottish lordship. Hansard’s “Lord Saltoun” of 1955 was followed by “Lady Saltoun of Abernethy”.[^21] The Scottish holders were Ladies, not Baronesses: the peerage of Scotland has no such rank.

Debrett’s table for England now gives them a line of their own, “Lords of Parliament, Scotland”.[^22]

## Why it matters

The common objection runs: a baron is a peer, so a Scottish baron who is not a peer is no real baron. That is English usage. In Scotland the word never passed to the peerage, because the baronage already held it.

Stair warns that the title “should not be confused with a dignity in the peerage of Scotland”.[^23] That is right, and it is the point. A Scottish baron does not claim a rank of the peerage. He holds a dignity older than the peerage: the Act of 2000 preserves “the dignity of baron”, and in 1943 the Lyon Court found the minor barons “a ‘titled nobility’”.[^24]

## What the record does not say

- **No source gives the reason in terms.** The charters show the separation. That the rank took its name from it is our reading.
- **The word still crosses over.** Debrett’s table for Scotland reads “Barons or Lords of Parliament (Scotland)”.
- **How far the Torphichen peerage follows the land.** Riddell: the barony “passed like a common property, under a limitation applicable to land, carrying the Peerage along with it”, and “a curious question might, possibly, arise, on the alienation” of the portion that carries the title. Stevenson: “When the holding was alienated the dignity went with it”, and Torphichen “is possibly an exception” to the rule that a dignity “is not now capable of transference”. Both leave the question open; the record shows the peerage passing with the barony by inheritance.
- **The phrase is older than the first named creations.** The Act of 1428 has “lords of parliament”; the first named creations are of the 1440s.
